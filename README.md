# Social Media and Political Attitudes among University Students: A Quantitative Analysis

## Project Overview

This repository presents a quantitative research project conducted during my undergraduate studies in Political Science at Aristotle University of Thessaloniki, Greece.

The study examines university students' perceptions of the relationship between social media, political information, political attitudes, and voting behaviour. It explores associations between these perceptions and investigates whether responses differ by gender.

## Data and Methodology

The analysis is based on questionnaire responses collected between December 2023 and January 2024 from 50 students enrolled in the Department of Political Sciences at Aristotle University of Thessaloniki.

The questionnaire covered students' characteristics, social media use, political information, and perceptions of the political influence of social media.

Data were recorded and analysed using **IBM SPSS Statistics**, applying the following methods:

- **Descriptive statistics** to summarise respondents' characteristics and questionnaire responses.
- **Spearman's rank correlation** to examine associations between ordinal variables.
- **Mann–Whitney U test** to investigate differences between male and female respondents.

## Key Findings

The analysis identified two positive and statistically significant correlations:

- Perceptions of social media use for political information were associated with perceptions of its influence on political attitudes and behaviour (ρ = 0.595, p < 0.001).
- Perceptions of social media as the main source of political information were associated with perceptions of its influence on voting behaviour (ρ = 0.503, p < 0.001).

The Mann–Whitney U test found no statistically significant gender difference in perceptions of social media's influence on voting behaviour (U = 283.50, p = 0.552).

These findings concern respondents' perceptions and do not establish causal relationships.

## Limitations

The research is based on a relatively small sample of 50 students from a single university department, limiting the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, the questionnaire primarily measures respondents' perceptions rather than their actual voting behaviour.
